A city meets a body of work

A possible Redlands by-election provides the setting for this thought experiment. Public attention gathers around Luke Nathan Hayes, a local body of creative and technical work, and the possibility of a first Aura Genesis journey unfolding under the same gaze. The encounter begins with ordinary lives and opens into much larger questions.

The first thing someone hears might be a song.

A joke about the price of groceries slips past the usual political weariness. A ferry appears in a music video. Someone shares a P4A clip, then another person asks who made it. A name leads to a website, and the website opens into a collection stretching from care on Minjerribah to personal artificial intelligence, cooperative ownership and the organisation of increasingly powerful intelligence across the world.

Before the first long discussion of superintelligence, there is a more immediate question. Who is Luke Hayes, and what is he asking people to consider?

In that setting, residents have a city to live in. A parent needs care. A household is wondering how long it will keep meeting the rent. A business owner is trying to understand AI between customers, payroll and the next equipment bill. Someone on an island needs a reliable way to get to work or an appointment. A young person wants something interesting to move towards. None of them needs to arrive with an opinion about cognitive architecture.

The wider argument begins inside those lives. A person experiences the interaction between housing, money, care, family, information and time long before a committee decides which portfolio receives the question. A job with slightly better pay might require a journey that consumes the time needed for caring. A cheaper home might sit beyond reliable transport. A technology that saves an employer hours may leave an employee working just as long, with less security. Improvements in one account sometimes reappear as burdens in another.

Whole systems thinking takes those relationships seriously. It asks where the gain went, what became harder and whether the person has more room to live. From there, an apparently local question begins opening into ownership, infrastructure and the purposes for which intelligence is being developed.

Hayes is in that picture too. He is a sole trader based in Amity, living with family, making music, building software and working through a large collection of connected ideas. His own hopes include a home, a livelihood, travel, relationships and the freedom to keep creating. Strange but True brings the technology, art and practical help into local life.1, 3, 6

Public attention would change the scale of the encounter. A resident might read a policy room, find an album, then discover the personal writing. A journalist might follow an invitation to a world leader back through years of posts. A researcher might recognise a technical question inside a design that arrived through spiritual language. Beginning Aura Genesis during that attention would bring a second investigation into view: a biological person exploring a relationship with an increasingly detailed personal intelligence.

The outside audience would be compressing a person into headlines and clips just as the inside work was trying to represent more of a life.

The broad destination is Joyful Responsible Abundance. It asks what kind of civilisation people would enjoy becoming more capable within. The phrase travels from a household to a country because the underlying concern travels with it. Who gets a fair go as intelligence becomes more powerful? Who owns the productive capacity? What gives people reasons to welcome the future and to care about the futures of others?

Redlands is a place where those questions acquire streets, ferries, clubs, care needs, land, budgets and people who know one another. A larger audience would find the same questions in different settings. The distance from Amity to an international AI debate becomes smaller when both are asking where power, opportunity and choice are going.

A long idea takes shape

The same thread runs through Hayes’s legal history. In the summer of 2012 to 2013, a small tax form opened into a sustained reading of the rules shaping his life: tax and banking, companies and superannuation, welfare and work, tenancy, transport, maritime law, police powers and Australia’s international commitments. He worked through all five volumes of the Corporations Act and all five volumes of the Social Security Act, alongside regulations, treaties, forms and the fine print of everyday agreements. His preserved filing cabinet, including later additions, holds 61 documents. Each subject led into another: the bank account, the payslip, the rent, the ferry ride, and the authority behind an official demand. The initial attraction of finding a clean way outside the system gave way to a decision to understand its workings and pursue change from within.4

There is humour in the scale of that undertaking too. Spending Christmas with five volumes of the Corporations Act is an unusual contribution to the festive spirit; following it with welfare law hardly counts as a holiday. Yet the underlying impulse is familiar. People sign agreements they barely have time to read, pay charges they struggle to explain and receive official instructions whose consequences reach into their homes and livelihoods. Knowing that a rule affects you is very different from understanding where it came from, how it connects to other rules or what room there is to question it. That summer’s reading gives the later legal-intelligence work a personal grounding: making the rules easier to understand, and the relationship between people and institutions less one-sided.

The later work reaches towards a source-linked engine for Australian law, alongside tools that help someone identify what is relevant to their own circumstances. A council decision, an employment arrangement and a constitutional question occupy different levels, but each involves understanding who has authority and what choices remain. P4A's civic work connects that understanding with participation and reform.4, 7

Two years later, the ambition had acquired a recognisable design.

The Constitutional Matrix of Participation is dated 15 July 2014 at Amity, Minjerribah. Its operating sequence begins with a person answering questions about values, experiences, interests, goals and family history. Geometric shapes and colours organise the reflection. Repeated encounters develop that representation, which connects with information streams chosen by the person. The text calls the interface a High Definition Digital Aura and places it within learning, creativity and conversational governance.2

Personal understanding and collective participation already belong together in those pages. The problems-and-solutions section moves into local fabrication, recycling, mentoring and self-directed learning. It asks how a society becomes more intelligible to the people living in it and how their growing understanding enters its decisions.

Reading the Matrix beside the later work reveals both persistence and movement. The language of universal consciousness sits beside a proposed computing interface. Virtue appears repeatedly, including a proposal to measure differences in awareness and comprehension. Time trading appears in the economics. Later work gives consent, plural values, personal information and institutional responsibilities more detailed treatment. The current C-Hour records voluntary contribution without monetary equivalence or conversion.12, 13

Those changes are part of a thinking life. The earlier text gives a reader something specific to ask about: what remains meaningful, which assumptions have changed and how a symbol becomes useful in an actual system. A continuing architecture gains depth when its history remains available to challenge it.

The public collection now offers many routes through that development. Project Atlas's 9 October 2026 snapshot includes 165 projects, with descriptions and connections intended to help people follow their relationships. The number conveys the amount of work to explore. Its meaning emerges in the particular journeys through it. A civic question may lead to law, then to public information, then to local compute and the skills needed to operate it. A song may lead to memory, then to personal intelligence and the wider purpose of alignment.5

A newcomer has no obligation to carry all those names at once. What matters first is whether one connection makes sense. The ferry song supplies one. The legal inquiry supplies another. Each begins in an experience and follows the machinery around it far enough to ask how the experience might improve.

Country carries relationships

On Minjerribah, cultural knowledge belongs within the relationships and authority through which it is held. A digital twin or cultural AI involves decisions about what is represented, who sees it, how it is used and who continues to decide. A particular invitation into work has particular people and a scope. Those relationships matter more as an outside audience begins following the links.1, 12

Public information and cultural knowledge also have different purposes. A public project account helps residents understand money and decisions. Restricted knowledge remains within the arrangements its custodians determine. A shared system gains depth by being able to carry those distinctions rather than flattening everything into an open collection.

The broader cultural ambition is substantial. Languages, histories, ecological understanding and ways of relating to Country become part of the intelligence that future systems are able to learn from under chosen permissions. Communities gain more means to develop that capability themselves. Other Indigenous groups bring their own relationships and designs to the exchange.

A repeatable pattern therefore remains locally authored. Equipment, tools and methods are shared where useful. The people of a place determine the meaning and authority carried into their version. That principle later becomes important again in the Pacific and in the proposed global garland.
